# Behavioral Interviews

Almost every onsite loop ends with a behavioral round, and candidates lose offers there more often than they expect: it is the round people prepare least and it is judged as closely as the coding rounds. It is also the cheapest round to prepare for, at roughly three hours of work. This page covers the method: how the round works, the STAR structure, your self-introduction, the recurring question categories with a question bank to drill against, the company research that feeds your answers and your questions, and what to ask at the end, sorted by who is asking you.

## What the round is

Thirty to sixty minutes with your prospective manager, a teammate, or a cross-functional interviewer. Instead of "solve this", you get "tell me about a time when...". The interviewer is assessing how you work with people: how you handle conflict, failure, ambiguity, and ownership, and how clearly you can describe all of it. Answers draw exclusively on your real past projects, so the preparation is about surfacing and structuring what you have already done.

## Answer with STAR

STAR is the standard structure for behavioral answers:

| Part | What it covers | Rough share |
|------|----------------|-------------|
| **S**ituation | The project and context, one or two sentences | 10% |
| **T**ask | What you specifically were responsible for | 10% |
| **A**ction | What you did, decided, or changed, step by step | 70% |
| **R**esult | The outcome, ideally with a number, and what you took away | 10% |

The weight sits on Action. Most weak answers spend too long on situation and too little on what the candidate personally did. Keep the whole answer near two minutes, use "I" rather than "we" for your actions, and finish with the result even when the interviewer nods: an answer without a result is a story without an ending.

## Prepare your self-introduction

The loop almost always opens with "tell me about yourself", and it sets the interviewer's expectations for every round after it. Prepare about 90 seconds:

1. What you work on now, in one sentence.
2. One or two things you are proud of, with a concrete outcome each.
3. Why this role or company, in one sentence.

Rehearse it aloud until it sounds like you talking, not a document being recited. It is the only answer you will give in every interview you ever take; the rehearsal cost is tiny. For where this fits in the round itself, see [The Coding Interview, Phase by Phase](../foundations/interview_practices.md).

## The recurring question categories

Behavioral question banks run to hundreds of items, but they collapse into a handful of categories:

| Category | Typical form |
|----------|--------------|
| Teamwork | Tell me about a time you worked closely on a team |
| Conflict | Describe a disagreement with a coworker or manager and how you resolved it |
| Failure | Tell me about your biggest mistake or a project that failed |
| Leadership | Tell me about a time you led a project or stepped beyond your role |
| Ambiguity | Tell me about a time you had to act without complete information |
| Pressure | Tell me about a time you delivered under a tight deadline or shifting requirements |
| Growth | Tell me about feedback that was hard to hear and what you did with it |

Prepare five to seven stories from real work, and write each one down in STAR form. Choose stories that flex: a good one answers two or three categories (the hard deadline is also the leadership story). Reusing a story across categories is expected; every interviewer in a loop hearing the exact same story is not, so know which of your stories are interchangeable.

## The common-questions bank

These are the questions that actually come back, three per category, with the one thing each is really scoring. Practice them aloud against your STAR stories; every answer draws on the [STAR structure](#answer-with-star) above.

**Teamwork**

- **Tell me about a time you worked closely with a team to ship something.** Pick a project where your contribution is specific; the collaboration is the setting, your actions are the story.
- **Tell me about a time a teammate was not pulling their weight.** Show curiosity before judgment: what you learned about their situation first, then what you changed.
- **Tell me about working with someone whose style differed from yours.** Name the concrete friction (review speed, level of detail), not a personality sketch, and the adjustment you made.

**Conflict**

- **Describe a disagreement with a coworker and how you resolved it.** Disagree about the work, stay respectful in the telling, and end with the relationship intact.
- **Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager.** Show how you made the case with evidence and committed fully once the call was made; disagree-and-commit is the phrase interviewers listen for.
- **Tell me about feedback you received that you initially disagreed with.** The strong version shows you sitting with it before responding, then either acting on it or building a calm case.

**Failure**

- **Tell me about your biggest professional mistake.** Own it plainly, skip the excuse, and spend most of the answer on the fix and the guardrail you added afterward.
- **Tell me about a project that failed.** Pick a real failure with stakes; a humble-brag reads as one, and what you changed afterward is the point.
- **Tell me about a decision you would take back.** A judgment probe: show that your decision process changed, not just that the outcome was unlucky.

**Leadership**

- **Tell me about a time you led a project.** Define leadership by what you unblocked and delegated, not by title; say what the team did, then what you did.
- **Tell me about a time you influenced without authority.** The classic senior probe: convince with data and framing, and credit the person you convinced for the final call.
- **Describe a time you went beyond your job description.** Anchor it in the team's need rather than heroics, and give the outcome a number.

**Ambiguity**

- **Tell me about a time you had to act without complete information.** Say explicitly what was unknown, which assumption you picked, and how you would have corrected course.
- **Tell me about a time priorities changed mid-project.** Show triage: what you stopped, what you kept, and how you renegotiated the deadline.
- **Describe a time you had to learn something quickly to deliver.** Name the timebox and the shortest honest path to competence: docs, a spike, a colleague who had done it before.

**Pressure**

- **Tell me about a time you delivered under a tight deadline.** Show scope-cutting, not overnight heroics; what you chose to drop matters as much as what shipped.
- **Tell me about handling competing urgent requests.** Show how you made the tradeoff visible to both stakeholders instead of silently picking one.
- **Tell me about an incident or fire you handled.** Walk the timeline: mitigation first, then root cause, then the follow-up that prevents the repeat.

**Growth**

- **Tell me about the hardest feedback you have received.** Pick feedback that stung and was right; the recovery arc is what is being scored.
- **Tell me about a time you gave difficult feedback to someone else.** Show care in delivery and a follow-up; the goal you acted toward was their improvement, not your own release.
- **What is a technical skill you did not have two years ago?** Trace the deliberate path: what triggered it, how you planned it, and evidence it stuck.

## Research the company before the loop

An hour of research pays out twice: it sharpens your answers ("why this company" stops being generic), and it generates the questions that make you look prepared at the end of every round. The checklist:

- **Use the product for thirty minutes** if you can, and note one thing you would improve and one thing done well; first-hand impressions beat press quotes.
- **Read the last three engineering-blog posts.** They name the stack, the scale, and the problems the team currently thinks about.
- **Search for the recent news:** funding, launches, leadership changes, layoffs. Know which season the company is in before you join it.
- **Read the critical takes too:** review sites and forum threads. Know the standard criticisms and form your own view of them.
- **Look up your interviewers** when you have their names, and draw one genuine question per person from their work or writing.
- **Map the job description to your stories:** mark which of your STAR stories proves each listed requirement, and patch the gaps before the loop.
- **Write down two or three numbers you can cite:** users, scale, revenue, team size. One well-placed number shows the research happened.

## Questions to ask at the end

"Think of a time you..." ends with "what do you want to ask us?" Have three to five real questions ready; "no, I'm good" reads as disinterest. The questions that land depend on who is in the room, so prepare for the four interviewer types below and pick as the loop unfolds.

### The recruiter screen

The recruiter owns process, logistics, and usually the compensation band, so process and money questions belong here:

- What does the rest of the process look like, and how far apart are the stages?
- Is this role a backfill or new headcount, and how long has it been open?
- What is the compensation band for this level, so neither of us wastes time?
- When would you want the person who takes this role to start?

### The hiring manager

This is the person whose mind you most need to change; ask about the team's shape and what success looks like:

- What would you want to see from me in the first six months?
- How are technical decisions made here, and who makes them?
- What does a normal week look like for the team I would join?
- What is the hardest part of this role?

### A prospective teammate

Peers can answer honestly about the work itself, which is what the manager round cannot always give you:

- What has the team shipped recently that you are proud of?
- What is the code and design review process like?
- What is onboarding like for someone joining now?
- Why did you join, and what has kept you?

### A cross-functional interviewer

Product managers, designers, and data partners sit across a different axis: how engineering relates to everyone else:

- When engineering and product disagree, how does the decision get made?
- How early does engineering see requirements, and how much can it shape them?
- Which other team does yours depend on most, and how does that relationship go?
- What one thing would make the collaboration between your side and engineering easier?

Tie any question back to something said earlier in the conversation when you can; it doubles as evidence you listen. Compensation questions stay with the recruiter and the offer stage, where [Salary Negotiation](salary_negotiation.md) picks them up.

## How to practice

1. Write your self-introduction and your STAR stories. That is most of the
   three-hour budget.
2. Rehearse aloud, never in your head. Spoken answers feel different from
   written ones, and the difference is the point.
3. Fold behavioral rounds into your [mock interviews](mock_interviews.md):
   after the coding mock, spend ten minutes on two or three "tell me about a time" questions and ask the partner to interrupt and probe, the way real interviewers do.
