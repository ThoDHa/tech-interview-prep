# Salary Negotiation

The loop ends and the offer arrives, and most candidates stop negotiating right at the moment negotiation actually starts: the initial offer is almost never the best package the company can make, and recruiters expect candidates to push back. This page covers what to do in that window: the rules of the conversation, the phrases that carry them, and how to evaluate what you are actually being paid. None of it takes more than an evening to apply.

## Understand the package first

A software offer is a stack of parts that behave very differently, and "which part should I push on" is the first negotiation question:

| Component | What it is | How negotiable |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| Base salary | Fixed cash per year | Moves, usually within a band; every future raise compounds on it |
| Signing bonus | One-time cash at start | Often the easiest cash to add; it does not compound |
| Equity | Stock or options, vesting over years | The company's favorite lever; refreshers depend on performance after you join |
| Annual bonus | Target percentage of base | Usually fixed by level; worth knowing, rarely worth fighting |
| Everything else | Remote status, start date, level, team, learning budget | Frequently worth more than cash, and cheaper for the company to give |

Two structural facts drive the whole page: base salary compounds through every year and every future raise, while a signing bonus is one-time money; and equity is the easiest thing for the company to grant because it burns no cash and aligns you with the company's outcome. Negotiate the stack, not a single number.

## The ten rules

These rules hold whether you have one offer or five. They reduce to one posture: stay warm, stay undecided, and keep every option open until you deliberately close it.

1. **Get everything in writing.** Note down every promise from every recruiter call, monetary or not; you will forget details that later matter ("we are porting the front end next year" is a negotiation fact).
2. **Never negotiate against yourself.** Do not reveal your current salary or your number first, and do not gush about any single component of the offer; comment on details only to clarify them.
3. **Keep the door open.** Your power lasts exactly as long as no decision is made; deflect every attempt to get you to commit ("when can you sign?") until you are genuinely ready.
4. **Stay positive about the company.** Excitement is an asset you keep spending: reiterate that you like the mission and the team even while the numbers are not working, or the recruiter stops fighting for you.
5. **Do not be the decision maker.** "I need to discuss this with my partner" makes you unpressurable; the recruiter cannot bully someone who is not in the room.
6. **Have alternatives, and make them visible.** Nothing moves an offer like a competing one; tell every company in your pipeline when one lands, and overlap your offers into one window.
7. **Give a reason for every ask.** Any honest reason ("commute to the city center eats two hours", "I am carrying student debt") makes the request human and gives the recruiter something to advocate with.
8. **Optimize more than cash.** Team, first project, mentor, start date, and level are all negotiable, and the non-cash ones are often where the real quality of life is.
9. **Understand what they value.** Push where giving is cheap for them (equity, signing bonus) and know that base moves in bands; asking the recruiter what flexibility exists beats guessing.
10. **Be winnable.** Give a clear path to yes: a package you would sign today, stated plainly. Playing games past a certain point just makes the company move on.

## Phrases that carry the rules

Negotiation is mostly a small set of sentences used calmly. These cover the recurring moments:

- **Deflecting the number question:** "I am interviewing with a few companies and want to keep the process fair to all of them, so I would rather discuss numbers once I have offers in hand."
- **Deflecting current salary:** "My current compensation is structured differently enough that a direct comparison would mislead both of us; I am targeting the market range for this level at your company."
- **Receiving the offer, staying warm:** "Thank you, I am genuinely excited about this team and the problem you are working on. I would like to review the full package and come back to you with questions."
- **Asking for time:** "When do you need a decision? I want to give you a proper answer rather than a fast one."
- **The ask itself:** "I am excited about this role. To get to yes, the base would need to reach [number], or the signing bonus could bridge the gap if base is fixed. Which levers do you have?"
- **Naming the competitor:** "I have an offer from [company] that is stronger on base, but I would rather join you; if we can close the gap, I will sign here."
- **Closing:** "If the revised offer comes back at [package], I am ready to sign this week."

Every phrase does the same two things: it restates interest, and it moves the decision one step away from "right now". That combination is the whole technique.

## Evaluating the offer

Before negotiating, decide what "better" means. Four steps:

1. **Benchmark the level, not the company.** Look the band up on [Levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi) for the company and the level, and sanity-check the total against the market. An offer can be generous for one company and low for its level.
2. **Price the risk.** Startup equity is a lottery ticket priced into your salary; compare offers on cash-plus-realistic-equity-value, not on the headline total. A public-company RSU grant at a known price is worth its face value times your belief in the stock.
3. **Compute the hourly rate.** A 20% salary premium for a 50% longer week is a pay cut. Divide by realistic hours, including on-call, before comparing.
4. **Score the non-cash columns separately.** Remote policy, learning budget, on-call load, commute, manager quality, and growth path do not fit the total, so give them their own tally. Cash gets you in the door; these decide whether you stay.

Then set your target from the data: a specific total and, more usefully, a specific acceptable shape ("base at least X, or base Y plus signing Z"). A number you can defend is the difference between negotiating and hoping.
